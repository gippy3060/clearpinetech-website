Clinic email security in BC: protecting patient information

Medical clinics · 8 November 2026

Email is the quiet weak point in many BC clinics. A front-desk employee gets a convincing "your fax has arrived" message, a physician's mailbox is taken over, or a referral letter with patient details goes to the wrong recipient because autocomplete picked the wrong name. Clinics hold some of the most sensitive information there is, so a small email mistake can become a privacy incident. This guide sets out practical email controls for a medical, dental, allied health or veterinary practice. It is general information, not legal advice. Check current requirements from your regulatory college, Doctors of BC or your professional association, and the Office of the Information and Privacy Commissioner for BC for obligations specific to your practice.

The clinic email risks that matter most

  1. Misdirected messages. Patient information sent to the wrong person, often through autocomplete or "reply all". This is the most common email privacy incident in a busy office and it needs no attacker at all.
  2. Account takeover. A phishing link or reused password lets someone sign in as a staff member, read patient email and send convincing messages from a trusted address.
  3. Fake invoices and supplier changes. A message claiming to be from a lab, equipment supplier or landlord asks you to pay a new bank account.
  4. Ransomware delivery. A malicious attachment or link is the usual way in. Our article on five common clinic security gaps covers what happens next.

Step 1: keep patient information out of ordinary email where you can

The safest patient email is the one you do not send. Most EMR systems have a secure messaging or patient portal feature, and referrals can often go by secure e-fax or the EMR's own transmission tools. Decide, as a practice, what may be sent by ordinary email (appointment reminders with no clinical detail, for example) and what must go through a secure channel. Write the rule down so every employee gives the same answer. If a patient asks you to email test results, explain the risk, record their consent in line with your privacy policy, and use a secure option where you have one. See also our guide to secure e-fax for BC clinics.

Step 2: multi-factor authentication for every mailbox

Require multi-factor authentication (MFA) for every user, including physicians, locums, assistants and part-time staff, on the web, desktop and mobile apps. Prefer an authenticator app or security key to text-message codes where practical. Turn off older sign-in methods that bypass MFA, a setting that older Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace tenancies sometimes still allow. Shared mailboxes such as "reception@" should not have a single shared password; give each person their own sign-in with delegated access, so you can see who did what.

Step 3: stop your domain being impersonated

SPF, DKIM and DMARC are three DNS records that tell receiving servers which senders are legitimate for your domain. Many small practices have none of them or only the first. Configured properly, they make it much harder for someone to send mail that appears to come from your clinic, and they give you reports on who is sending mail as you. Roll DMARC out in stages, starting with monitoring, so legitimate systems such as appointment-reminder tools and billing software are not blocked. Ask your IT provider to show you the current records.

Step 4: filtering and warning banners

  • Anti-phishing filtering that checks links and attachments on delivery and again when clicked.
  • External sender tags so a message from outside the practice is clearly marked.
  • Impersonation protection for the names of physicians, the practice manager and anyone who approves payments.
  • Blocked risky attachment types and protected view for Office files from outside the clinic.

Filters are a safety net. Some messages will still get through, which is why the process steps below matter.

Step 5: a simple rule for payment and banking changes

Any request to change where a payment is sent, or to pay a new supplier, should be confirmed by calling a phone number you already had on file, never one supplied in the message. Apply the rule to everybody, including physicians and the practice owner. Urgency is a warning sign, not a reason to skip the call.

Step 6: prevent the misdirected-email mistake

  • Turn off or clean up the autocomplete cache so old and mistyped addresses do not appear as suggestions.
  • Use a delay on outgoing mail (even 30 seconds) so a mistake can be recalled.
  • Pause before "reply all" on any message that includes clinical detail.
  • Keep a short written procedure for what to do if something goes to the wrong person: recall it if possible, ask the recipient to delete it, record what happened, and tell your privacy officer straight away.

Step 7: watch for a quietly taken-over mailbox

Ask your IT provider to review, and ideally alert on, new inbox rules that forward mail outside the practice, sign-ins from unusual places, unexpected changes to MFA methods and new connected apps nobody approved. Alerts only help if someone reads them, so agree in advance who receives them and what they do next.

Step 8: short, regular staff reminders

A once-a-year slide deck is quickly forgotten. Short, regular examples work better: a fake fax notification, a fake "shared document", a fake supplier invoice. Make reporting easy and blame-free. A staff member who reports a click within minutes is far more valuable than one who hides it for a week.

If you think something has gone wrong

  1. Act immediately. Reset the password, sign out active sessions and confirm the MFA methods belong to the user.
  2. Remove any forwarding rules and connected apps the user did not create.
  3. Work out what was accessed using the audit logs, so you know whose information may be affected.
  4. Involve your privacy officer and your insurer early. Cyber coverage often requires prompt notice.
  5. Consider your reporting duties. Depending on what was exposed, you may have obligations to patients, your regulatory college or the privacy commissioner. Get advice rather than guessing.
  6. Make sure you have a working backup of the EMR and files. See our guide to EMR backup best practices for BC clinics.

A quick checklist

  • A written rule for what may and may not go by ordinary email.
  • MFA on every mailbox, legacy sign-in disabled, no shared passwords.
  • SPF, DKIM and DMARC configured and reviewed.
  • Anti-phishing filtering, external sender tags and impersonation protection.
  • Call-back rule for payment and banking changes.
  • Autocomplete cleanup and an outgoing-mail delay.
  • Alerts on forwarding rules and unusual sign-ins.
  • Short, regular staff reminders and a written incident plan.

Frequently asked questions

Is Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace secure enough for a clinic?

Both can be when configured properly, but default settings are rarely enough. Most incidents come from missing MFA, weak policies and unreviewed settings rather than a flaw in the platform. Also confirm where your data is stored and what your privacy policy tells patients.

Can we email patients at all?

Many clinics do for low-risk matters such as appointment reminders. For clinical detail, use a secure portal or messaging feature where you can, and follow the guidance of your regulatory college and your privacy policy.

We are only a small practice. Is this overkill?

Small practices are targeted precisely because they tend to have fewer controls. Most of the steps above are settings and habits rather than expensive tools.
