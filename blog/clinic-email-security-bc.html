Clinic email security in BC: protecting patient information in Microsoft 365

Medical clinics · 7 November 2026

Email is where most small clinics are attacked. A receptionist opens a fake invoice, a physician’s password is reused from another site, or a mailbox quietly starts forwarding messages to a stranger. Because clinic email often carries referrals, lab results and patient questions, one compromised account can become a privacy incident. This guide covers practical, mostly low-cost steps for BC clinics, with Microsoft 365 as the example because many practices use it.

We are an IT provider, not your regulator or lawyer. Your college, professional association, privacy officer and legal counsel can tell you what applies to your practice, including any breach-reporting duties under PIPA or PIPEDA; this is the technical side.

Why clinic email is a target

  • It holds sensitive information. Even a short message can name a patient and a condition.
  • Busy front desks open attachments. Fake invoices, fake fax notifications and fake “shared document” links look routine.
  • Accounts are shared. A single “reception@” password known by everyone cannot be traced or revoked cleanly.
  • Small teams have no security staff. Settings stay at their defaults for years.

Seven steps that make the biggest difference

  1. Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) for everyone. A password alone is not enough. Use an authenticator app or security key rather than text messages where you can, and include physicians, locums and the shared reception login.
  2. Give every person their own account. Use shared mailboxes for things like referrals or bookings, but have each person sign in as themselves. Remove accounts the day someone leaves.
  3. Block automatic forwarding to outside addresses. Attackers who get into a mailbox often add a forwarding rule so they keep receiving mail after the password is changed. Staff forwarding to personal Gmail has the same effect. Review existing rules at least once.
  4. Turn on anti-phishing and attachment filtering. Microsoft 365 includes baseline protection and higher tiers add more. Check what your current licence includes.
  5. Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC for your domain. These records make it harder for someone to send email that appears to come from your clinic. Your IT provider or domain host can check them in a few minutes.
  6. Keep patient documents out of ordinary attachments where practical. Share files through a protected link with an expiry, or through your EMR’s own messaging, rather than attaching them to emails that sit in inboxes indefinitely. Confirm the recipient address before sending anything sensitive.
  7. Teach staff three habits. Pause on unexpected attachments and urgent requests, check the sender’s real address, and report anything odd without fear of blame. A short reminder every few months works better than one long annual session.

What not to email

Decide as a team what should never travel by ordinary email: full charts, scanned ID, or anything your clinic’s privacy policy says needs a more secure route. Write it on one page and put it where staff will see it. If patients ask you to email them results, have a standard explanation of the risk and a record of their consent, in line with your college’s guidance and your privacy officer’s advice.

Phishing that targets clinics

  • “You have a new fax” emails with a link or attachment. Open faxes from your portal or EMR instead. See secure e-fax for BC clinics.
  • Fake supplier invoices or changed banking details. Confirm by phone using a number you already have.
  • Messages that appear to come from a colleague asking for gift cards, a quick favour or a password.
  • “Your mailbox is full” or “password expires” messages leading to a lookalike sign-in page.

If you think an account has been compromised

  1. Change the password and sign the user out of all sessions right away.
  2. Check for new forwarding rules, inbox rules that hide messages, and unfamiliar devices or app connections.
  3. Make sure MFA is on, and reset the second factor if the account was fully taken over.
  4. Review the sign-in and mailbox audit logs to see what was accessed. Not every licence keeps the same log history, so it helps to know this before an incident.
  5. Tell your privacy officer promptly. Whether and how to report is a decision for them, your regulator or your lawyer, informed by what the logs show.
  6. Warn contacts if messages were sent from the account, and tighten the settings above.

Backups for email too

Cloud email is not automatically a backup. Deleted items, retention periods and what you can restore vary by plan. Ask what is recoverable, for how long, and who can restore it. Our EMR backup guide covers the clinical system; email and shared files deserve the same questions.

Quick checklist

  • MFA enabled on every account, including shared and physician accounts
  • No shared passwords; leavers removed the same day
  • External auto-forwarding blocked and existing rules reviewed
  • SPF, DKIM and DMARC published for the clinic domain
  • Anti-phishing and attachment filtering confirmed
  • One-page “what not to email” rule and a way to report suspicious messages
  • Known answer to “what can we restore, and for how long?”
