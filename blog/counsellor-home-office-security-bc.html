How to secure a home office for BC counsellors and therapists

Counselling practices · 13 November 2026

A lot of counselling now happens from a spare bedroom. Registered clinical counsellors, psychologists, social workers and therapists in BC routinely see some or all of their clients by video from home, and many group practices have clinicians who split their week between a shared office and a home office. The home office is where privacy safeguards are most likely to slip: a family computer, a router nobody has updated since it was installed, a door that does not quite close. This guide sets out practical steps to secure a home office for client sessions and records. It is general technical information, not legal or clinical advice. Your regulatory college, professional association and your own counsel are the right people to confirm what applies to your practice.

Why the home office needs its own plan

Private practices in BC are generally subject to the Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA), which expects reasonable security arrangements for personal information in your custody. Counselling notes, assessments and even the fact that someone is a client are about as sensitive as personal information gets. A safeguard that is fine in an office (a shared printer down the hall, a desktop behind a locked door) can look very different at home, where other people live, devices are shared and nobody is managing the network.

The risks we think about for a home office fall into four groups:

  1. Being overheard or overseen. Family members, roommates, visitors, smart speakers and windows facing the street.
  2. Shared or unmanaged devices. Client records opened on a family laptop, saved to a personal Downloads folder or synced to a personal cloud account.
  3. The home network. An old router with a default password, other people's devices on the same Wi-Fi, and gadgets that rarely get security updates.
  4. Lost or stolen equipment. A laptop taken in a break-in or left in a car, with no encryption and no way to wipe it remotely.

Step 1: a device used only for practice work

The single most useful change is a computer that is used for client work and nothing else. It does not need to be expensive. What matters is that nobody else in the household uses it, it is not loaded with games, browser extensions and personal accounts, and it can be managed to a written standard. If clinicians use their own laptops, agree in writing what the device must meet before it touches client records (see the checklist below).

On that device:

  • Turn on full-disk encryption (BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on a Mac) and keep the recovery key somewhere the practice can retrieve it.
  • Give each person their own sign-in. No shared "Office" account, no blank passwords.
  • Set the screen to lock automatically after a few minutes of inactivity, and get into the habit of locking it whenever you step away.
  • Keep automatic updates on for the operating system, browser and video software, and restart when prompted rather than postponing for weeks.
  • Run endpoint protection that someone actually monitors, so an alert is noticed rather than dismissed.
  • Enrol the device in management so a lost or stolen laptop can be locked or wiped.

Step 2: keep client records in the practice system, not on the laptop

Most counselling practices use cloud practice-management software for booking, notes and billing. The safest home office keeps client information inside that system rather than scattered across the computer. In practice that means:

  • Write notes directly in the practice platform rather than in a Word document you paste in later.
  • Avoid downloading intake forms, reports and letters to the Downloads folder. If you must, delete them once they are uploaded, and empty the recycle bin.
  • Turn off syncing of work files to personal cloud accounts (personal OneDrive, iCloud Drive, Google Drive or Dropbox).
  • Do not save the practice platform password in a browser that the family also uses. Use a password manager on the practice device instead.
  • Use multi-factor authentication on the practice platform, email and video accounts. An authenticator app is generally a stronger choice than text-message codes.

If your practice keeps files outside the platform, such as scanned forms, assessment reports or a shared drive, they need their own backup. Our guide to EMR backup best practices for BC clinics covers the principles, and they apply equally to counselling records.

Step 3: secure the home network

You do not need enterprise equipment at home, but the basics matter:

  • Change the router's admin password from the default printed on the sticker, and keep the router's firmware updated. If the router is old enough that it no longer receives updates, replace it.
  • Use WPA2 or WPA3 encryption on the Wi-Fi with a long passphrase. Turn off WPS.
  • Put guests and smart devices on a separate network. Most current routers offer a guest network. Smart TVs, doorbells, cameras and visitors' phones can live there, away from the practice laptop.
  • Prefer a cable for sessions. A wired Ethernet connection to the practice computer is usually more stable for video than Wi-Fi in a room at the far end of the house. If that is not possible, test the connection in the room you actually use, at the time of day you see clients.
  • Avoid public Wi-Fi for client work. If you sometimes work away from home and office, a phone hotspot is generally a better choice than a café or hotel network.

Some practices go further and provide a practice-managed firewall or access point for clinicians who see clients from home regularly. That gives the practice a consistent, documented setup rather than relying on whatever the internet provider supplied.

Step 4: make the room private

Technology is only part of a private session. A few physical checks go a long way:

  • Choose a room with a door that closes, away from shared walls where possible. A white-noise machine outside the door helps.
  • Use a headset so the client's side of the conversation is not on a speaker.
  • Check what your camera shows behind you. Family photos, mail and documents on a shelf can reveal more than you intend. A plain background or a blur setting is simplest.
  • Unplug or mute smart speakers and voice assistants in the room, and turn off voice activation on the practice computer.
  • Position the screen so it cannot be seen from a doorway or window.
  • Keep paper to a minimum. If you print or take handwritten notes, store them in a locked drawer or cabinet and shred them when they are no longer needed under your records policy.

Step 5: set up the video platform properly

Which video platforms are acceptable for your profession is a question for your college or association. Whatever you use, the account settings matter as much as the product:

  • Use an account the practice controls, protected with multi-factor authentication, not a free personal account.
  • Use unique meeting links or a waiting room so a client cannot walk into someone else's session.
  • Leave recording off unless you have a deliberate reason, client consent and somewhere appropriate to store the recording.
  • Keep the video app and browser updated.
  • Have a short fallback plan for when video fails: who calls whom, and on which number.

Step 6: email and messaging from home

Home is where shortcuts creep in: forwarding work email to a personal address so it is easier to read on the phone, or texting a client from a personal number. Keep practice email in the practice account, with multi-factor authentication, and keep sensitive content out of ordinary email unless you have a secure method. Our guide to clinic email security in BC sets out what to send, what not to send and how to handle a misdirected message.

If you use a phone for practice work, give it a strong passcode, keep it updated, turn on encryption and remote wipe, and keep client conversations inside apps the practice has approved.

Step 7: plan for a lost laptop or a break-in

It helps to decide in advance what happens if a practice device goes missing:

  1. Report it to the practice owner or IT provider the same day.
  2. Lock or wipe the device remotely and sign it out of the practice platform, email and video accounts.
  3. Change passwords for any accounts that were signed in.
  4. Record what happened, what information may have been on the device and whether it was encrypted.
  5. Consider with your advisors whether the incident needs to be reported to clients or a regulator. Encryption is often the difference between a near miss and a serious breach.

A home-office checklist for clinicians

  • Practice-only device, encrypted, with its own sign-in and automatic screen lock
  • Automatic updates and monitored endpoint protection
  • Device enrolled in management so it can be locked or wiped
  • Multi-factor authentication on practice software, email and video accounts
  • Client records kept in the practice platform; no personal cloud sync
  • Router admin password changed, firmware current, WPA2/WPA3 Wi-Fi
  • Guests and smart devices on a separate network
  • Private room, headset, neutral background, smart speakers off
  • Paper locked away and shredded under the records policy
  • A written plan for a lost or stolen device

For group practices, the same checklist doubles as the standard for contract clinicians who use their own equipment. Agree it in writing at onboarding, and remove access on their last day.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use my family computer for client sessions if I log out afterwards?

It is better not to. Logging out does not stop files from being downloaded, passwords from being saved or malware installed by another user from watching what you type. A separate device used only for practice work is far easier to secure and to explain if something goes wrong.

Do I need a VPN at home?

If your practice software, email and video platform are cloud services that already use encrypted connections, a VPN adds less than people expect. It matters more when you need to reach something inside the office network, such as a file server. Device security, multi-factor authentication and a secured home network usually come first.

Our clinicians are contractors using their own laptops. What can the practice require?

Many practices set a written device standard (encryption, updates, screen lock, endpoint protection, no shared use) and require it before granting access to client records. How that fits with your contracts is a matter for your advisors; we can help write the technical standard and check devices against it.
