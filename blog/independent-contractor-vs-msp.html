Independent IT Contractor vs. a Managed Service Provider: What a Growing Business Is Actually Trading Off

IT support comparison · 28 September 2026

A lot of small businesses in the Fraser Valley start their IT relationship with one person — a contractor a founder knows, or found through a referral, who handles the network, fixes the printer, and answers the phone when something breaks. For a five-person office, this works, and it's usually cheaper than anything else on the table. The question worth asking isn't whether that was the right call at the time — it almost always was — but what changes as the business grows, and at what point the arrangement starts costing more than it saves.

The single point of failure problem

The core structural issue with an independent contractor isn't skill — plenty of solo contractors are excellent technically. It's that the business's entire IT function runs through one person's availability. If they're on vacation, sick, or simply juggling another client's emergency, there's no backup with context on your environment. For a business with no compliance obligations and low IT dependency, that risk is tolerable. For a clinic that can't see patients without its practice management system, or an office that can't process payroll without network access, a single person being unreachable for a day is a real operational exposure, not a minor inconvenience.

An MSP's core value proposition is that the client's environment is documented well enough that more than one technician can pick up a ticket. That documentation has a cost — it's part of why MSP retainers run higher than a contractor's hourly rate — but it's what turns "our IT person is out" into a non-event.

Response time is a function of capacity, not intent

Most independent contractors genuinely intend to respond fast. The limiting factor is that they're one person with a finite number of hours, often serving ten or more clients. When two clients have emergencies at the same time, one waits — and there's no dispatch process to route the work, just whoever the contractor picks up first. An MSP with even a small team can run parallel: one technician on a ticket while another handles a call. That's not a knock on contractors' work ethic; it's math.

Scope creep and vague accountability

A common pattern with contractor arrangements is that the scope of work was never precisely defined — "handle our IT" covers a lot of ground, and it tends to expand quietly as the business adds users, software and vendors. Without a documented service scope, it's genuinely hard to tell whether something isn't being done because it's out of scope or because it's been missed. This isn't usually anyone acting in bad faith — it's what happens when the working relationship was built on trust and a handshake rather than a written agreement with a defined boundary. A managed IT agreement forces that boundary into writing: what's covered, what's billed separately, and what the response-time commitment actually is.

Where contractors still make sense

None of this means every growing business needs to switch immediately. A contractor remains the better economic choice when the business is small enough that downtime for a day is a real but manageable inconvenience, when the IT footprint is simple (a handful of laptops, cloud email, no on-prem servers or compliance requirements), and when the relationship is genuinely responsive in practice, not just in theory. The mistake is assuming the arrangement that worked at 8 employees will still work at 35, or that it will scale automatically with the business — it usually doesn't, because the contractor's available hours don't grow, even if the client list does.

The signals worth watching for

A few patterns tend to show up before a business consciously decides to switch: tickets or calls taking noticeably longer to get a response than they used to; the same recurring issue being patched rather than fixed, because there's never time for the underlying project work; no real answer when asked "what's our disaster recovery plan" beyond "I have a backup somewhere"; and a growing unease about what happens if this one person becomes unavailable. Any one of those on its own isn't a crisis. Two or three together, in a business that now has real compliance exposure or real revenue at stake if systems go down, is usually the point where the math on a managed IT provider starts to make more sense than it did a year or two earlier.
