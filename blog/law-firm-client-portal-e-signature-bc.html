Secure client portals and e-signatures for small BC law firms

Law firms · 26 November 2026

Most small law firms now send drafts, collect documents and get signatures without a client ever coming into the office. That is convenient for everyone, but it also means confidential client information moves through more systems than it used to: inboxes, file-sharing links, a client portal, an e-signature service and the client's own phone. Each one is a place where a document can go to the wrong person, sit around longer than it should, or be faked. This guide explains how a small or mid-sized BC firm can choose and set up a client portal and an e-signature process that protect confidentiality without making life harder for clients. It is general information about IT practice, not legal advice. Check current Law Society of BC rules and guidance, your professional liability insurer's conditions and the requirements for each type of document you sign.

Why plain email attachments are not enough for everything

Email is fine for many routine messages, and plenty of clients prefer it. The trouble with attachments for sensitive material is practical:

  • Copies multiply. An attachment ends up in your sent items, the client's inbox, any device they read mail on, and sometimes a spouse's or employer's account they forward it to. You cannot take any of those copies back.
  • Mistakes are permanent. Autocomplete picks the wrong "John", or a reply-all includes opposing counsel. Once it is sent, it is out of your control.
  • No record of access. You cannot tell whether the client opened the document, or whether someone else did.
  • Fraud thrives on it. Fake "shared document" emails and altered PDFs are a common route into a firm, and clients who are used to receiving attachments from you are easier to fool with a forged one.

A client portal or an expiring secure link does not remove every risk, but it keeps the document in one place you control, lets you revoke access, and leaves an audit trail. For a wider look at email risks, see our guide to email security for BC law firms.

Three ways to share documents securely

Most firms end up using a mix of the following. What matters is that each lawyer knows which to use when.

  1. The portal in your practice-management software. Many cloud practice-management products include a client portal where clients can see shared documents, upload their own and send messages. The big advantage is that everything stays with the matter. If your software has one, it is usually the first option to consider.
  2. A secure file-sharing link. Business file-sharing tools, including the sharing features in Microsoft 365, can send a link that requires the recipient to sign in or enter a one-time code, expires after a set period, and can block downloads. This works well for large files and for people who will never log in to a portal, such as opposing counsel or an expert.
  3. Encrypted email. Some email platforms can send a message that the recipient opens through a secure page. It feels familiar to clients, but it is less organised than a portal for an ongoing matter.

Avoid sending client files through personal file-sharing accounts, consumer messaging apps or a link that "anyone with the link" can open with no expiry. Those are convenient, but they leave no meaningful control once the link is forwarded.

Choosing a portal: questions to ask

Whether you use a portal built into your practice software or a separate product, the due diligence is similar to vetting any cloud service that holds client information. Our article on vetting Clio and other cloud practice software against Law Society expectations covers this in depth. For a portal specifically, ask:

  • Where are documents stored, and can you choose Canadian data storage if that matters to you or your clients?
  • How do clients sign in? Look for unique client accounts, a verification step on first sign-in and the option of multi-factor authentication (MFA) for clients, not just staff.
  • Can you control access per matter? A client with two matters, or a matter with two parties who should not see each other's documents, needs separate access.
  • Is there an audit log showing who viewed, uploaded or downloaded each document and when?
  • Can you remove access quickly when a matter closes, a retainer ends or a client relationship breaks down?
  • How are uploads handled? Files from clients should be scanned for malware before staff open them.
  • How do you get everything out? If you change software, documents and messages need to come back to your file with their history intact.

Setting up a portal so it actually protects confidentiality

Most portal problems come from setup and habits rather than the product. A sensible baseline:

  • MFA for every staff account that can publish to the portal, with the same rules as your email and practice software.
  • Verify the client before granting access. Send the invitation to an email address you confirmed during intake, and confirm by phone if the client asks to change it. A request to "send the portal invite to my new address instead" is a classic fraud pattern.
  • Share deliberately. Make "not shared" the default for documents in the matter, so nothing appears to the client until a lawyer or assistant chooses to share it.
  • Watch the names on shared items. File names are often visible to the client. "Draft v3 - partner comments - weak points" should not be.
  • Close access at the end. Build a step into your file-closing checklist: remove portal access, revoke open links, and move the final documents to your closed-file archive. Our document management guidance covers where closed files should live.
  • Tell clients how you communicate. A short note in your retainer letter or welcome email, saying "we will only ever share documents through our portal, and we will never change payment details by email", makes forged messages much easier for a client to spot.

E-signatures: what the service does, and what it does not

E-signature services save a great deal of time, but it helps to be clear about what they prove. A typical service records the email address the request went to, the time of each step, and often an IP address and device details, then seals the completed document so later changes can be detected. That is useful evidence that whoever controlled that email account signed the document. It is not, by itself, proof of who that person is.

Keep three questions separate:

  1. Can this document be signed electronically at all? Many documents can, but some documents, filings and registrations have specific execution, witnessing, commissioning or filing requirements. Check the applicable legislation, Law Society guidance and the receiving court or registry before relying on an e-signature for a particular type of document.
  2. Have you verified the client's identity? Client identification and verification is its own obligation under the Law Society rules, with its own accepted methods. An e-signature audit trail does not replace it.
  3. Is the signer the person you verified? This is where IT controls help. Send signature requests only to contact details confirmed at intake, and where the service offers it, add a second check such as a one-time code sent by text message to a phone number you already have on file.

Setting up e-signatures securely

  • Use a firm account with named users, not one shared login. Each lawyer or assistant who sends documents has their own account with MFA, so the audit trail shows who sent what.
  • Have one approved service. When every lawyer uses a different free tool, nobody knows where signed client documents are stored. Pick one, review its security and data location once, and make it the firm standard.
  • Save the completed document and certificate to the matter. The signed copy and its completion certificate belong in the client file, not only in the e-signature service's dashboard. Set up automatic saving to the matter where your tools allow it.
  • Use templates for routine documents such as retainer agreements and authorizations, so fields and recipients are set consistently and nobody sends a draft by mistake.
  • Remove departing staff the same day. E-signature and portal accounts are easy to forget when someone leaves.
  • Check connected apps. If the e-signature service connects to your practice software, email or cloud storage, review what access that connection has and remove connections nobody uses.

Spotting fake portal and signature requests

Attackers copy the look of popular portal and e-signature emails because people are used to clicking them. Teach staff, and tell clients, to watch for:

  • A signature or "secure document" request that nobody at the firm was expecting, or that comes from an address that is close to, but not the same as, a real one.
  • A sign-in page that asks for an email password before showing a document. Genuine e-signature requests normally open without asking for your email password.
  • Urgency around payments: "sign the attached and send funds today". Any change to payment instructions should be confirmed by calling a number you already had on file.
  • A client saying they received a document from you that you did not send. Treat that as a possible compromised mailbox and act on it; see cyber incident response for BC law firms.

Remote and hybrid work

Portals and e-signatures make it easy to work from anywhere, which is exactly why the devices used to reach them matter. Staff should publish client documents only from firm-managed devices with up-to-date software, disk encryption and a screen lock, not from a home computer shared with family. If your firm is setting this up for the first time, the IT setup checklist for a new BC law firm covers devices, accounts and backups.

A quick checklist

  • A written rule on when to use the portal, a secure link or ordinary email.
  • One approved portal and one approved e-signature service, reviewed for data location, access control, audit logs and data export.
  • MFA on every staff account for both, and on client portal accounts where offered.
  • Client contact details confirmed at intake and changes verified by phone.
  • Documents "not shared" by default; file names checked before sharing.
  • Identity verification handled separately from e-signatures.
  • Signed documents and completion certificates saved to the matter.
  • Portal and link access removed as part of file closing and staff offboarding.
  • Clients told, in writing, how the firm will and will not contact them.

Frequently asked questions

Is emailing documents to clients a breach of confidentiality?

Not automatically. Email is widely used and clients often prefer it. The question is whether the method is reasonable for the sensitivity of the document and whether the client understands the risks. For sensitive material, a portal or expiring secure link is usually the better choice, and it gives you a record of who accessed what.

Which client portal should a small firm use?

Start with the portal built into the practice-management software you already use, if it has one. It keeps documents with the matter and avoids another system to secure. A separate portal or secure file-sharing service makes sense when your practice software lacks one or when you need features it does not offer. Either way, check where data is stored, how access is controlled and how you get your data out.

Are electronic signatures valid in BC?

Electronic signatures are widely accepted for many documents, but some documents and filings have specific execution, witnessing or filing requirements. Confirm the requirements for each document type with the applicable legislation, Law Society guidance and the receiving registry or court before relying on an e-signature.

Can an e-signature service replace client identity verification?

No. Signing a document and verifying who a client is are separate steps. An e-signature service may record an email address, phone number or IP address, but that does not by itself meet your client identification and verification obligations. Follow the Law Society's requirements for verifying identity, then use e-signatures for documents.
