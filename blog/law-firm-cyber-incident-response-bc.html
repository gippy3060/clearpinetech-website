Cyber incident response for BC law firms: the first 24 hours

Law firms · 16 November 2026

Most small law firms do not have an incident response plan. They have a phone number for "the IT person" and a vague sense that the insurer should be told at some point. Then a Monday morning arrives with encrypted files on the server, or an associate reports that their laptop was taken from a car, or a client calls to ask why your firm emailed them new wire instructions. Decisions made in the first few hours shape how much client information is exposed, how quickly the firm can work again and how the firm's response looks to clients, the insurer and the Law Society afterwards.

This guide walks through what a small or mid-sized BC firm should do in the first 24 hours of the three incidents we see discussed most often, and the short plan to write now so nobody has to improvise. It is general information, not legal advice. Your professional obligations, your insurance policy and privacy law may require specific steps, so involve your own counsel and insurer early.

First, what counts as an incident?

An incident is anything that may have put client or firm information at risk, or that stops the firm from working. That includes the obvious (ransomware, a hacked mailbox) and the easy to dismiss:

  • A lost or stolen laptop, phone or USB drive, even if "it had a password".
  • A staff member who entered their password on a fake sign-in page.
  • An email with client documents sent to the wrong person.
  • Unexplained sign-ins, new mailbox forwarding rules or MFA prompts nobody requested.
  • A vendor (practice-management, accounting, e-fax or cloud storage provider) telling you they had a breach.
  • A departing employee who kept access to systems or took files.

Make it clear to everyone in the firm that reporting a possible incident quickly is expected and will not be punished. The most expensive incidents are usually the ones someone sat on for a week.

The first hour: the same five steps for every incident

  1. Stop and write things down. Start a simple log: date, time, who noticed what, and every action taken from now on. Insurers, forensic investigators and regulators will ask for this timeline, and memories fade fast.
  2. Call your IT provider. Tell them what you know and that you are treating it as a possible security incident, not a routine ticket.
  3. Call your insurer's breach line before you clean anything up. Many cyber policies expect prompt notice and may require you to use their approved responders. Wiping and rebuilding a computer before they are involved can destroy evidence they need and, in some cases, affect coverage. Find out now what cyber coverage your firm actually has, including any coverage arranged through the Law Society's insurance program and any separate cyber policy.
  4. Contain, but do not destroy. Disconnecting an affected computer from the network is usually right. Turning it off, reinstalling it or deleting suspicious emails usually is not, until the responders say so.
  5. Decide who is in charge. One lawyer makes the decisions and handles communication with clients, the insurer and the Law Society. Everyone else routes questions through them.

Scenario 1: ransomware or encrypted files

Signs include files that will not open and have strange extensions, a ransom note on screen or in folders, and practice software that suddenly cannot find its data.

  • Isolate affected machines. Unplug network cables and turn off Wi-Fi on any computer showing symptoms. If the server or file share is affected, your IT provider may disconnect it from the network entirely.
  • Protect the backups. Make sure backup systems are disconnected from anything compromised, and that nobody "restores" over the top of the evidence. A backup copy that attackers cannot change or delete is what turns a disaster into an inconvenience; see why your backups are probably not working.
  • Do not contact the attackers or pay without your insurer, counsel and responders. Payment raises legal, ethical and practical questions, and paying does not guarantee your data is returned or deleted.
  • Assume data may have been copied, not only locked. Many ransomware groups take a copy before encrypting. The investigation should establish what was accessed, because that drives your obligations to clients.
  • Work out how the firm keeps practising. Cloud practice-management and email may still work from clean devices. Court deadlines and closings do not move, so identify urgent matters early and who will cover them.

Scenario 2: a lost or stolen laptop or phone

  • Report it the same day to IT and, for theft, to police. Get a file number.
  • Lock and, if appropriate, wipe it remotely. A managed device can be locked or wiped from the management console. An unmanaged personal phone with firm email on it usually cannot, which is a good reason to manage them.
  • Revoke the user's sessions and change their password. Signing the device out of email, practice-management software and cloud storage closes the door even if the device is never recovered.
  • Establish whether it was encrypted. Full-disk encryption (BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on Mac), confirmed as switched on before the loss, is often the difference between a near miss and a reportable breach. "It had a Windows password" is not the same thing.
  • List what was on it. Files saved locally, downloaded attachments, synced folders. If client files lived only on that laptop, you also have a recovery problem, not just a privacy one.

Scenario 3: a taken-over mailbox or payment fraud

Typical signs: clients receive odd messages from your address, replies arrive to emails you never sent, a bank or client questions a change of payment instructions, or you find forwarding rules nobody created.

  • Lock the account down: reset the password, sign out all sessions, check the MFA methods belong to the user, and remove forwarding rules and connected apps they did not set up.
  • If money may have moved, call the bank immediately. Speed matters most for recalling a transfer. Then check trust account activity carefully and involve your accountant.
  • Warn affected clients by phone, from numbers you already had on file, not by replying to email threads that may be compromised.
  • Pull the audit logs to see what the attacker could read. A mailbox that was open for weeks can expose every matter that person worked on.

Prevention for this one is covered in detail in law firm email security in BC, including the call-back rule for payment instructions.

Hours 2 to 24: obligations and communication

Once the immediate damage is contained, the focus shifts to who needs to be told and what the firm says.

  • The Law Society. The Law Society of BC Rules require a lawyer to notify the Law Society promptly when records may have been lost or accessed without authorization, and to take reasonable steps to protect clients' interests. Read the current rule and any Law Society practice resources on the subject, or ask the Law Society's practice advisors, rather than relying on a summary.
  • Clients. Clients whose confidential information may be affected generally need to know, and your professional duties to them sit alongside any privacy-law duty. Counsel and your insurer's breach coach can help with timing and wording.
  • Privacy law. BC's Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA) and, in some situations, federal privacy law may set expectations or requirements for notifying individuals and the privacy commissioner. Get advice on which applies to your firm and the information involved.
  • Staff. Tell them what happened in plain terms, what to say if a client calls, and not to discuss it on social media or with outside parties.

Keep adding to the incident log throughout. When things settle, it becomes the basis of your report to the insurer and of the lessons-learned review.

Write a one-page plan now

A useful incident plan for a small firm fits on one page and lives somewhere you can reach when your own systems are down: printed in the office, and saved on a phone. It should list:

  • Who leads: the responsible lawyer and a backup.
  • Phone numbers: IT provider (including after hours), insurer's breach line and policy number, bank fraud line, Law Society practice advice, your own counsel.
  • Where things are: which systems hold client data, who your vendors are, and where backups are kept.
  • The first five steps from this article, and a blank incident log template.
  • How the firm keeps working: which matters are urgent, how staff can work from clean devices, and how clients will be reached.

Review it once a year and whenever key people, vendors or insurers change. A 30-minute tabletop exercise ("the server is encrypted on a Tuesday, what do we do?") will show the gaps faster than any document review.

Controls that make the 24 hours easier

Most of what decides how bad an incident gets is in place, or not, long before it happens:

  • MFA on email, practice-management software, remote access and banking.
  • Full-disk encryption and remote wipe on every laptop and phone that touches client data.
  • Backups with at least one copy attackers cannot alter, plus a restore you have actually tested.
  • Audit logging switched on in your email tenancy and kept long enough to investigate.
  • Prompt removal of access when someone leaves.
  • A list of vendors and the contracts that say what they must tell you after a breach. Our guide on vetting Clio and other cloud practice software against Law Society expectations covers the questions to ask.

A quick checklist

  • Everyone knows how to report a possible incident, and that it is safe to do so.
  • A one-page plan with phone numbers, printed and on a phone.
  • Cyber coverage confirmed and the breach line number recorded.
  • Devices encrypted and manageable remotely.
  • Backups isolated and restore-tested.
  • Email audit logging on; MFA everywhere.
  • A tabletop exercise in the last 12 months.

Frequently asked questions

Should we just restore from backup and carry on?

Not before your insurer and responders have had a chance to look. Restoring can overwrite evidence of how the attacker got in and what they accessed, and if the entry point is not closed, the same thing can happen again a week later.

Our laptop was encrypted. Do we still need to do anything?

Yes: record the loss, revoke the device's sessions, confirm encryption was really on (your IT provider can usually check this from the management console) and get advice on whether any notification is still needed. Encryption greatly reduces the risk but the firm still needs a record of how it handled the loss.

Can our IT provider handle the whole incident?

Your IT provider should contain the problem, preserve logs, restore systems and support the investigation. Legal advice, decisions about notification and, often, forensic investigation sit with your counsel, your insurer and the responders they appoint. A good provider will expect to work alongside them.
