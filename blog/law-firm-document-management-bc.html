SharePoint, Clio or a DMS? Document management for small BC law firms

Law firms · 22 November 2026

Ask the staff of a small law firm where the latest draft of a client's agreement is, and you'll often get three answers: "in Clio", "on the S: drive" and "I emailed it to myself". Every one of those places may hold part of the client file. That makes documents slow to find, hard to back up properly, awkward to lock down when a conflict screen goes up and painful to hand over when a matter closes or a lawyer leaves the firm.

Most small and mid-sized BC firms end up choosing among three approaches: the document storage built into their practice-management software, a structured Microsoft 365 setup (SharePoint and Teams), or a dedicated legal document management system (DMS). This guide compares them in plain terms, explains what each one asks of you, and gives you a set of questions for deciding. It's written from the IT side. It isn't legal advice, and what the Law Society of BC expects of your practice is a question for your own counsel and the Law Society's practice advisors.

Why "where do files live?" matters more than which product you buy

Every option below can work well. Firms run into trouble when they never decide, so documents end up wherever each person finds convenient. The practical problems are the same whatever software you use:

  • Confidentiality. If files sit in personal OneDrive folders, inboxes and Downloads, you can't say who has access to a client's documents, and you can't remove access cleanly when someone leaves.
  • Conflict screens. When a lawyer has to be walled off from a matter, you need a way to block their access that holds up. "Please don't open that folder" isn't one.
  • Backups and recovery. Your backup only protects what it can see. A document saved to a laptop desktop or a personal cloud account is usually outside it.
  • Completeness. When a file is transferred, produced or closed, you want the whole file, including the email and the scans, not just the parts that happened to be filed.
  • Retention. Closed files have to be kept for a period and then destroyed deliberately. That only works if you know where they are.

So the first decision isn't "SharePoint or a DMS?". It's "we will keep every client document in one place, and here's that place". The product choice follows from that.

Option 1: your practice-management software's document storage

Cloud practice-management platforms such as Clio, Actionstep and LEAP include document storage tied to each matter. Locally installed products such as PCLaw are typically used alongside a file share or another document system, so check what your own product does before assuming.

Where it fits well: sole practitioners and small firms that already live in their practice-management software every day, have modest document volumes and want one less system to manage. Documents sit next to the matter, time entries and billing, which makes them easy for staff to find.

What to check:

  • How documents are edited. Many platforms open Word files through a desktop sync tool or an add-in. Find out where the working copy sits while it's open, and what happens if two people edit at once.
  • Version history, and whether you can restore an earlier version yourself.
  • Search, especially full-text search inside scanned PDFs, which depends on text recognition (OCR) being applied.
  • Permissions. Can you restrict a single matter to named users for a conflict screen, or is access all-or-nothing by role?
  • Email filing. Is there an Outlook add-in that saves messages and attachments to the matter, and do people actually use it?
  • Export. If you leave the platform, how do you get every document out, with its folder structure and matter association intact?

The questions in our guide to vetting Clio and other cloud practice software against Law Society expectations apply here too: where data is stored, how the vendor secures it, and what you're still responsible for configuring.

Option 2: Microsoft 365 (SharePoint and Teams)

Most small firms already pay for Microsoft 365 for email. Its business plans include SharePoint, the document library service that sits behind Teams and shared OneDrive folders. A well-planned SharePoint setup can make a solid document system for a small firm. A poorly planned one recreates the old messy shared drive, just in the cloud.

Where it fits well: firms with a handful to a few dozen people who want co-authoring in Word, decent search and access from anywhere, and who are willing to set up a structure and stick to it.

What good looks like:

  • A deliberate structure. For example, a document library per practice group, with a folder per client and matter that follows the matter number from your practice-management software. Avoid creating a new Team for every matter unless someone is managing that sprawl.
  • Permissions set at the library or matter level, not file by file. Broken-out permissions on individual documents quickly become impossible to audit. Matters behind a conflict screen, family and employment files, and firm HR records belong in their own restricted locations.
  • External sharing locked down. Sharing with clients and opposing counsel should use links limited to specific people, with expiry where you can set it, and never "anyone with the link". Your IT provider can set these defaults for the whole tenant.
  • No personal OneDrive as the system of record. OneDrive is fine as a personal scratch space, but final copies belong in the matter library.
  • Version history on and staff shown how to restore an earlier version.
  • Retention planned, not assumed. Depending on your licence, Microsoft 365 offers retention policies and labels. These need careful setup, because a badly scoped policy can either keep everything forever or delete things you needed.
  • A separate backup. Microsoft keeps the service running, but it doesn't promise to recover data your own users or an attacker deleted or encrypted beyond its short recycle-bin and version windows. A third-party Microsoft 365 backup closes that gap. We cover why in your backups are probably not working.

The trade-off: SharePoint isn't legal software. It doesn't know what a matter is, it won't profile documents by client and matter number, and email filing takes more discipline than in a dedicated legal tool. Some firms bridge this with integrations between their practice-management software and SharePoint. Test these before you rely on them.

Option 3: a dedicated legal document management system

Legal DMS products are built around the client-matter structure. Documents are "profiled" with a client, matter, document type and author. Email can be filed to a matter from Outlook in a click or two. Ethical walls are a built-in feature rather than a permissions workaround. They're common in larger firms, and some are offered at prices aimed at smaller ones.

Where it fits well: firms with heavy document volumes, many precedents, lots of email to file, several lawyers working on each matter, or regular conflict screens. These are firms where the time lost hunting for documents is clearly costing money.

What to check:

  • Per-user cost and minimum seats, on top of your practice-management and Microsoft 365 licences.
  • Integration with your practice-management software, so matters don't have to be created twice.
  • Where the data is hosted, the vendor's security documentation, and how you'd export everything if you left.
  • The effort of migration. Moving years of loosely organised files into a profiled system is real work, and it's usually best done matter by matter, starting with open files.
  • Training. A DMS only pays off if everyone files into it, including partners.

Questions to settle before you choose

  1. How many documents and emails does a typical matter generate? A few dozen favours simplicity. Hundreds per matter favours a DMS.
  2. How often do you need conflict screens or restricted matters? If it's routine, test how each option handles it before you commit.
  3. Where do you spend your day? If everyone lives in the practice-management software, its storage may be the path of least resistance. If everyone lives in Outlook and Word, Microsoft 365 or a DMS with a good Outlook add-in will get more consistent use.
  4. Who will own the structure? Someone has to create matter folders or workspaces, set permissions and archive closed files. If that's nobody, choose the option that does most of it automatically.
  5. How will scanned documents arrive? Decide where the scanner sends files and who files them. It shouldn't be an open network folder or a personal email address.
  6. What happens when a matter closes? Decide where closed files go, how long they're kept and who approves destruction.
  7. How will you get out? Every platform should have a tested way to export all your documents in a usable form.

Security settings that apply whichever option you choose

  • Individual accounts for every person, with multi-factor authentication. No shared logins.
  • Managed, encrypted laptops and phones, so a lost device doesn't expose the synced files on it. See our guide to responding to a lost device or other incident.
  • Sync limited to what people need, rather than whole libraries copied to every laptop.
  • Same-day removal of access when staff, students or contract lawyers leave, plus a review of what they shared externally.
  • A backup of documents that's independent of the platform, with a restore tested at least once a year.
  • Audit logging turned on, so you can see who opened or downloaded a file if you ever need to.
  • Careful email practice around documents: attachments to the right recipient, and secure links for anything sensitive. Our law-firm email security guide covers this.

Moving without losing anything

Whichever way you go, a migration goes more smoothly if you:

  1. Take a full backup of the current locations before anything moves.
  2. Agree on the new structure and naming on paper first, with one example matter set up and checked by a lawyer and an assistant.
  3. Move open matters first, then closed matters still within their retention period, and leave the old location read-only for a set period rather than deleting it straight away.
  4. Check permissions on restricted matters after the move, not just before.
  5. Run short, hands-on training on filing email, scanning and sharing externally, because those are the habits that decide whether the new system stays tidy.

A one-page checklist

  • One written answer to "where do client documents live?"
  • A matter structure that follows your practice-management matter numbers
  • Restricted locations for conflict-screened, family, employment and HR files
  • A consistent way to file client email to the matter
  • External sharing limited to named people, with expiring links
  • Scanner sending to a protected, filed location
  • Version history on, and staff know how to restore
  • An independent backup with a tested restore
  • A closed-file archive and a documented retention and destruction process
  • A tested way to export everything if you change platforms

Frequently asked questions

Is SharePoint good enough for a small law firm?

For many small firms, yes, provided it's set up with a clear matter structure, permissions at the library or matter level, locked-down external sharing and a separate backup. It's weaker than a legal DMS at email filing, document profiling and ethical walls, so firms that depend heavily on those should compare both.

Can we keep documents only in Clio or our practice-management software?

Often, yes, especially for sole practitioners and small firms. Check matter-level permissions, version history, email filing and export before you rely on it, and make sure there's a copy of your documents outside the platform that you control.

Do we need a document management system to meet Law Society of BC expectations?

We're not aware of any requirement to use a particular kind of product. What matters is that client information is kept confidential, secure and retrievable, and that you can show how. Confirm your obligations with the Law Society's practice advisors or your own counsel.

What's wrong with our old shared drive on the office server?

Nothing, if it's well organised, permissioned, backed up off site and reachable securely when people work remotely. In practice, many older shared drives have none of those, and moving to the cloud is a good moment to fix the structure as well.
