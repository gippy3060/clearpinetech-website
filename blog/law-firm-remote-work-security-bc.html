Remote work security for BC law firms: a practical checklist

Law firms · 13 November 2026

Most small BC law firms now have at least one lawyer or assistant who works from home some of the time. A sole practitioner may draft at the kitchen table in the evening; a five-lawyer firm in Surrey or Langley may have staff on a hybrid schedule; a paralegal may finish a closing from a laptop on the weekend. That flexibility is useful, but every home office is another place where client files can be seen, copied, lost or stolen. This guide sets out what a small or mid-sized firm should put in place so remote work does not quietly weaken the confidentiality you owe clients. It is general information, not legal advice. Check the Law Society of BC's current rules and practice resources, your professional liability insurer's conditions and your own counsel for obligations specific to your practice.

Why remote work is a different risk for a law firm

Your duty to keep client information confidential does not change when the work moves out of the office. What changes is how much of the environment you control. In the office, you chose the network, the locks, the printer and who walks past the screen. At home, a lawyer might share a Wi-Fi router with teenagers' gaming consoles, print a draft on a family inkjet, take a call within earshot of a roommate, or use a personal laptop that also runs a child's school apps. None of these is dramatic on its own. Together they create the gaps that lead to lost files and awkward conversations with clients and insurers.

The good news is that most of the fix is a small set of decisions made once, written down, and applied to everyone, including partners.

1. Decide which devices may touch client files

The most important remote-work decision is whether people can use personal computers. There are two workable approaches:

  • Firm-issued laptops only. The firm buys, configures and manages every laptop used for client work. This is the simplest to secure and the easiest to explain to an insurer. When someone leaves, the device comes back and is wiped.
  • Personal devices with limits. If staff use their own computers, restrict them to browser access to your practice-management and email systems, block downloading and syncing of client files to the personal device where your software allows it, and require a supported, updated operating system and screen lock.

What tends to go wrong is the unplanned middle ground: a personal laptop with a full copy of the firm's document folder synced to it, shared with family members, with no way to remove the data if the person leaves. If that describes your firm today, it is the first thing to fix.

2. Encrypt and manage every laptop

Laptops get left in cars, coffee shops and courthouse hallways. Full-disk encryption (BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on Mac) means a lost laptop is an inconvenience instead of a potential breach of every file on it. Alongside encryption, firm laptops should have:

  • A screen lock after a short idle period and a strong sign-in (PIN or biometric backed by a strong password).
  • Automatic operating system and application updates, with someone checking that they actually install.
  • Endpoint protection that reports back to whoever manages your IT, so an infection is noticed rather than discovered months later.
  • Device management that lets the firm lock or wipe a laptop remotely if it is lost or an employee leaves.
  • Separate standard user accounts, with administrator rights reserved for IT, so a malicious download cannot quietly install itself.

3. Make multi-factor authentication non-negotiable

Remote access means your systems are reachable from anywhere, which means a stolen password is usable from anywhere. Require multi-factor authentication (MFA) on email, your practice-management system (Clio, PCLaw, Actionstep, LEAP or whatever you use), document storage, accounting and any remote desktop connection. Prefer an authenticator app or security key over text messages. Our article on law firm email security covers MFA and phishing in more detail.

4. Choose a sensible way to reach firm systems

How remote staff connect depends on where your files live:

  • Cloud practice-management and document systems. If your matters, documents and billing are in a cloud platform, staff can usually work in a browser or app with MFA, and there is no need to open a path into the office network. Do the due diligence on the platform itself; see how to vet Clio and other cloud practice software against Law Society expectations.
  • On-premises server or desktop software. If you still run a server or a desktop application such as an older PCLaw installation in the office, remote staff need a secure route in: a properly configured VPN or a remote desktop gateway, both protected by MFA. Never expose remote desktop directly to the internet; it is one of the most common ways ransomware gets into small offices.

Remote work is often the push a firm needs to decide whether to keep an aging office server or move to cloud tools. That is worth an honest conversation about cost, risk and how your practice actually works, rather than a default either way.

5. Secure the home network well enough

You cannot audit every lawyer's house, but you can set a reasonable baseline and put it in writing:

  • Change the default administrator password on the home router and keep its firmware updated. Replace very old routers that no longer receive updates.
  • Use WPA2 or WPA3 Wi-Fi encryption with a strong passphrase.
  • Where the router allows it, put work devices on a different network from smart TVs, game consoles and other household gadgets, or at least use a guest network for the gadgets.
  • Avoid public Wi-Fi for client work. A phone hotspot is usually the safer option at a courthouse or café.

Because the firm's own systems require MFA and encryption, the home network does not have to be perfect. It just should not be the weakest link.

6. Paper, printing and conversations

Many remote-work incidents are physical rather than technical:

  • Printing. Decide whether home printing of client documents is allowed. If it is, documents need to be stored securely and shredded, not put in the household recycling. Many firms simply say "print at the office".
  • Files taken home. Paper files should travel in a locked bag and be stored out of sight, not on the dining-room table.
  • Calls and video meetings. Take client calls where others cannot hear, use headphones, and check what is visible on camera and on screen. Smart speakers in the room are worth turning off during confidential calls.
  • Screens. Lock the screen whenever stepping away, even at home.

7. Keep client files out of personal accounts

When systems are awkward, people improvise: they email a document to their personal Gmail to print it, save a draft to a personal Dropbox, or text a photo of a signed page from a personal phone. Each of these puts client information somewhere the firm cannot see, secure or delete. The cure is partly policy and partly making the approved route easy. If staff routinely work around a system, fix the system, then make the rule clear: client information stays in firm-managed accounts and storage.

8. Back up what lives outside the main system

Cloud practice-management platforms back up their own data, but that rarely covers everything. Remote staff often create files outside it: local drafts, scanned documents, downloaded disclosure, email in a desktop mail client. Make sure files are saved to firm storage that is backed up, not to a laptop's desktop, and that your email and document storage have a backup separate from the provider's own recycle bin. Test restoring a file now and then. Our post on why your backups may not be working explains what to check.

9. Plan for lost devices and departing staff

Write down, in advance, what happens when:

  • A laptop or phone is lost or stolen. Who is told, how quickly it is locked or wiped, how you determine what was on it, and when your insurer is contacted.
  • Someone leaves the firm. Accounts are disabled the same day, MFA methods are removed, firm devices are returned and wiped, and any personal device access is revoked. Shared passwords they knew are changed.
  • Something looks wrong. Staff know who to call and that reporting quickly is expected and blame-free.

Depending on what was exposed, a lost device or compromised account may also trigger reporting duties to the Law Society, to clients or under privacy legislation. Get advice on that rather than guessing.

10. Put it in a one-page remote-work policy

A long policy nobody reads is less useful than one page everyone signs. It should cover which devices are allowed, MFA and encryption requirements, home network basics, printing and paper, where files are saved, what not to do with personal accounts, and what to do if a device is lost. Review it once a year, and whenever you change practice-management or email systems.

A quick remote-work checklist for BC law firms

  • Decision made and written down on firm-issued versus personal devices.
  • Full-disk encryption, screen lock, updates and endpoint protection on every laptop used for client work.
  • Remote lock and wipe available for firm devices.
  • MFA on email, practice management, document storage, accounting and remote access.
  • No remote desktop exposed directly to the internet; VPN or gateway with MFA if you have an office server.
  • Home router basics: changed admin password, current firmware, WPA2/WPA3, gadgets separated where possible.
  • Rules for printing, paper files and confidential calls at home.
  • Client information kept out of personal email and storage accounts.
  • Backups covering files and email outside the practice-management platform, with test restores.
  • Written steps for lost devices and departing staff, including who calls the insurer.

Frequently asked questions

Can lawyers in BC work from home?

Yes, many do. The question is not whether remote work is allowed but whether the firm's arrangements protect client confidentiality and meet its professional and insurance obligations. Check the Law Society of BC's current guidance and your insurer's conditions for specifics.

Is a VPN enough to make remote work secure?

No. A VPN protects the connection back to an office network, but it does nothing about an unencrypted laptop, a weak password without MFA, files saved to a personal account or a document left on a home printer. If your systems are in the cloud, you may not need a VPN at all; MFA, device management and encryption matter more.

Can staff use their own computers?

They can, with limits. Browser-only access to firm systems, no syncing of client files to the personal device, MFA and an updated operating system is a reasonable minimum. Firm-issued laptops are simpler to secure and to recover when someone leaves.

We are a three-lawyer firm. Do we really need all this?

Small firms usually need fewer tools, not fewer controls. Encryption, MFA, updates and a one-page policy cost little and cover most of the risk.
