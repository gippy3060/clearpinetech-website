How much does IT support cost for a medical clinic in BC?

Medical clinics · 27 November 2026

Ask three IT companies what support costs for a family practice or specialist clinic and you may get three answers that are hard to compare: one quotes per device, one per hour, one a flat fee with a long list of exclusions. This guide sets out our own pricing with the arithmetic shown, explains what changes the cost for a medical clinic in particular (the EMR hosting model, locums, exam-room computers and fax), and gives you questions to put to any provider. Clearpine is a small managed IT provider based in Abbotsford, so we will say where our pricing applies and where it does not.

Our pricing, stated plainly

Clearpine’s managed IT support is $95 per user per month, with a minimum of 5 users and a 1-year agreement. Support is delivered remotely during business hours. Three things are billed separately: Microsoft 365 licences, after-hours support and on-site visits. The exception: on-site visits are free if the problem can’t be fixed remotely.

A “user” is a person who logs in to clinic computers or accounts, not a computer and not an exam room. In a medical clinic that usually means the physicians, any nurse or nurse practitioner, medical office assistants (MOAs), the clinic manager and anyone else who uses the EMR, email or clinic workstations.

What changes the cost for a medical clinic

The support fee is per user, but the total you spend on IT each year depends on decisions specific to medical practice. These are the ones worth understanding before you compare quotes.

1. How your EMR is hosted

EMRs used in BC, such as OSCAR Pro, Accuro, TELUS PS Suite and Med Access, can be run in different ways depending on the product and the contract: hosted by the vendor in the cloud, hosted by a third party, or installed on a server in the clinic. The hosting model moves cost around more than any other single choice.

  • Vendor- or cloud-hosted. No clinic server to buy, back up or replace. Your spend shifts to a reliable internet connection (ideally with a backup line), well-managed workstations and strong logins. The EMR vendor handles the server; your IT provider still handles everything the clinic touches.
  • On a server in the clinic. You pay for the server, its warranty, a backup system with an off-site copy and, every few years, a replacement and data migration. Backups and restore tests matter far more; see EMR backup best practices for BC clinics.

Neither model is right for every clinic. If you are weighing a move, ask your EMR vendor for its hosting options and confirm where the data is stored, then ask your IT provider what work each option leaves with the clinic.

2. Locums, residents and changing staff

Medical clinics have more people coming and going than most small offices: locums covering holidays, residents and students on rotation, MOAs who move between clinics. Each one needs an account set up correctly on day one and removed on their last day, in the EMR, email, the e-fax service and any shared drive. That work is part of good support, and it is also a privacy safeguard: an account left open after someone leaves is a common way records end up seen by the wrong person. Ask any provider how they handle onboarding and offboarding, and whether it is included.

3. Exam-room and shared computers

Exam rooms often have a computer that several physicians use through the day. The safe setup is individual logins with a quick way to switch users and an automatic screen lock, so charts are not left open when a patient is alone in the room. Getting that right takes some configuration up front, but it avoids the far bigger cost of a shared password that everyone knows.

4. Fax, scanning and referrals

Many BC clinics still receive referrals, results and requests by fax. Whether you use a fax machine, a fax server or an e-fax service changes what needs supporting: phone lines, scanners, the e-fax account and how incoming documents reach the EMR. A misdirected fax is also one of the most common privacy incidents in a clinic. See secure e-fax for BC clinics for the options.

5. Equipment that connects to your network

ECG machines, spirometers, label printers, card readers and check-in tablets may connect to a workstation or the clinic network. Each one adds drivers, updates and occasional vendor calls. List them for any provider quoting your clinic so the quote reflects what you actually run.

What a monthly fee should cover

Whoever you hire, ask them to put this in writing. Two quotes that look far apart on price can cover very different work.

  • Help desk. Who answers when an MOA cannot log in to the EMR or a referral will not print, and during what hours?
  • Patching and monitoring. Are workstations, any server and the firewall kept up to date and watched, or only fixed after something breaks?
  • Backups. If you have anything in the clinic (an EMR server, scanned documents, shared files), is it backed up off site and test-restored?
  • Security basics. Multi-factor authentication, endpoint protection and email filtering are the baseline now, not extras. See email security for BC clinics.
  • Vendor liaison. Will the provider call your EMR, e-fax and equipment vendors so your clinic manager does not have to sit in the middle?
  • Accounts and access. Onboarding and offboarding for physicians, locums and staff, and a regular review of who can see what.
  • Documentation. An inventory of devices, accounts and settings stored where you can reach it if you ever change provider.

What is usually billed on top

The support fee is only part of what a clinic spends on IT. Budget separately for:

  • Microsoft 365 licences for email and documents, billed per user. With us these are billed separately from the $95 support rate.
  • EMR licences and vendor support, paid to your EMR vendor, plus any e-fax or secure messaging subscription.
  • Hardware: workstations, exam-room computers, printers and scanners, the firewall, switches, Wi-Fi and a server if your EMR runs on site.
  • After-hours work. Server replacements, EMR upgrades and network changes are often best done when the clinic is closed. With us, after-hours support is billed separately.
  • Projects such as a clinic move, a new location or an EMR migration.

Splitting IT costs between physicians

Many BC clinics are shared practices where physicians pay into common overhead. Per-user pricing makes that split easier to reason about: each physician’s own login is one user, and shared staff can be divided the same way the clinic already divides other overhead. Whatever method you use, write it into your cost-sharing arrangement so a new physician joining or a locum arriving does not reopen the conversation. Your accountant can advise on how the costs should be treated.

Remote support and the on-site question

Most clinic IT problems are not physical: a failed login, a printer queue that jams, a slow workstation, an update that went wrong or a backup warning. These can be fixed remotely, which is why remote support is the base of our service and keeps the monthly figure down. Some problems are physical, such as a dead switch, a failed drive or a computer that will not start. For those, on-site visits are free if the problem can’t be fixed remotely. Planned on-site visits you request are billed separately.

Privacy obligations and why they affect the budget

Private medical clinics in BC are generally subject to the Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA), and the College of Physicians and Surgeons of BC sets expectations for medical records. Your college, Doctors of BC and legal counsel can confirm exactly what applies to your practice. On the IT side, meeting those expectations comes down to practical safeguards: individual logins, multi-factor authentication, encrypted laptops, screen locks, current patches and backups that ransomware cannot reach. A cheaper plan that skips these is not cheaper if it leaves you unable to show reasonable safeguards after an incident. For how these map to day-to-day IT, read PIPEDA for clinics: what your IT actually has to do, and for the bad day itself, cyber incident response for BC clinics.

Downtime has a direct cost

If the EMR is unreachable, the schedule stops, prescriptions and referrals wait, and patients sit in the waiting room. Estimate what an hour without the EMR costs your clinic in lost visits, overtime and rework, then compare that with the monthly fee. That comparison is usually a better guide than the fee alone.

Questions to ask any IT provider

  1. What is the price per user, per device or flat, and what is the minimum term?
  2. Which items are billed separately: licences, after-hours work, on-site visits, projects?
  3. How do you count locums, residents and part-time staff?
  4. Will you work with our EMR vendor, and what do you do when a problem sits between the EMR and our computers?
  5. How do you set up shared exam-room computers so charts are not left open?
  6. If we keep anything on site, how are backups tested, and when did you last test-restore one?
  7. What happens if we leave: who holds the documentation and administrator accounts?
  8. Will you do the first assessment at no charge?

How to compare quotes fairly

Put every quote on the same basis. Convert it to a monthly cost for your actual headcount, then add what each provider bills separately. A low headline rate that leaves out security tools, backups or on-site help can cost more over a year than a higher rate that includes them. Check the contract term and how you leave, too. Frequently asked questions

How much does managed IT support cost for a medical clinic in BC?

With Clearpine it is $95 per user per month, minimum 5 users, on a 1-year agreement, delivered remotely during business hours. A clinic with 8 people who log in pays $760 a month. Microsoft 365 licences, after-hours support and on-site visits are billed separately, but on-site visits are free if the problem can’t be fixed remotely.

Does a cloud EMR make IT support cheaper?

A cloud-hosted EMR removes the clinic server, its backups and its replacement cycle, which usually lowers hardware and project costs. It does not remove the need for support: workstations, logins, multi-factor authentication, printers, scanners, fax, the network and email still need looking after, and the clinic still needs its own export and downtime plan.

Do locum physicians count as users?

Anyone who logs in to clinic computers or accounts is normally a user. How short-term locums, residents and part-time staff are counted is something to agree in writing before you sign with any provider.

Is EMR software included in the IT support fee?

No. EMR licences and vendor support are paid to the EMR vendor. Your IT provider supports the computers, network and accounts the EMR runs on and works with the vendor when a problem sits between the two.
