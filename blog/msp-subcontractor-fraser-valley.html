Why National MSPs Subcontract Local Coverage — and How It Works

Partner channel · 28 September 2026

Most national and regional MSPs run their service desk from one or two hubs — Calgary, Vancouver, Toronto — and that model works fine for remote monitoring, patching and ticket triage. It breaks down the moment a client needs a hand physically on a keyboard: a failed switch that needs replacing before Monday, a new hire's workstation that needs staging, a site move that needs bodies in the building overnight. Flying or driving a technician from head office to Abbotsford or Chilliwack for a two-hour job doesn't pencil out, and it makes the response-time commitment in the SLA nearly impossible to hit for that region.

The standard fix is a local subcontractor: a smaller, geographically anchored IT firm that the national MSP dispatches for on-site work under the MSP's own client agreement. The client generally never sees the subcontractor's name — they just experience a technician showing up on time, doing the work, and closing the ticket back in the MSP's system. This is common enough in the industry that most delivery managers already have one or two subcontractors on file for markets they don't cover directly; the question is usually whether they have one for this market.

What the relationship actually covers

In practice, subcontracted local coverage breaks into a handful of repeatable service types, not a vague "help us out sometimes" arrangement:

Dispatched smart hands and deskside support — a technician sent to execute specific, MSP-directed work: swap a part, reseat a cable, walk a remote engineer through a physical diagnosis, escort a vendor, reboot a rack. The MSP retains the ticket and the diagnosis; the subcontractor executes.

Hardware staging and deployment — unboxing, imaging, asset tagging and configuring workstations or network gear before rollout, either at the subcontractor's site or the client's.

Site rollouts and moves — a new office build-out, a relocation, or a multi-site refresh where the MSP needs a project-managed local presence for days or weeks, not a single dispatch.

Overflow service desk capacity — ticket volume during a big rollout or a short-staffed stretch that the MSP's own desk can absorb by routing a defined slice of tickets to the subcontractor.

How the SLA and dispatch process usually work

A subcontractor agreement is typically its own document, separate from the MSP's client-facing SLA, but built to make that client SLA achievable. It sets a response-time commitment specific to the subcontractor — often tighter than what the client sees, since it has to leave room for the MSP's own coordination time — and a defined dispatch channel: a ticketing integration, a shared inbox, or a phone line that's monitored during agreed hours. The cleanest arrangements specify exactly what triggers a dispatch, what information comes with it, and what "done" looks like.

Billing is usually a blended model: a standing retainer for guaranteed response-time coverage in the region, plus hourly or per-dispatch rates for actual work performed above a baseline. A pure time-and-materials arrangement with no retainer tends to produce worse response times, because the subcontractor has no incentive to prioritize the MSP's tickets over other work.

Vetting: what MSPs actually check

Before handing a subcontractor access to client environments, most MSPs verify a short list of things: general liability insurance (professional/E&O liability matters more if the subcontractor is doing any independent diagnosis rather than pure execution), a background-check policy for technicians who'll be on client premises, a documented process for handling credentials and access, and references from at least one comparable arrangement. For healthcare or government clients, expect an additional layer — confidentiality agreements, sometimes a criminal record check per technician, and clarity on where data touches the subcontractor's own systems versus staying entirely within the MSP's tools.

Why this matters in the Fraser Valley specifically

The Fraser Valley and the eastern Lower Mainland are an awkward gap for most national MSPs: close enough to Vancouver to look "covered" on a map, far enough that a same-day dispatch from a Vancouver or Calgary office eats the margin on every ticket. A subcontractor already based in Abbotsford closes that gap without the MSP opening a satellite office or carrying local headcount they can't keep utilized.
