Sharing IT with room-renting practitioners: a guide for BC multidisciplinary clinics

Multidisciplinary clinics · 28 November 2026

Many clinics in the Lower Mainland are really several small practices under one roof. A chiropractor owns the lease, two registered massage therapists rent rooms, a physiotherapist works three days a week as an independent contractor, and a counsellor or naturopath uses the back office on Thursdays. Everyone shares the front desk, the Wi-Fi, the printer and often the booking software. It's an efficient way to run a clinic, and it raises an IT question most owners never quite settle: who is allowed to see what?

When the answer is "everyone can see everything", the clinic has a privacy problem waiting to happen. When nobody owns the answer, accounts pile up, passwords get shared and nobody is sure what happens to a practitioner's records when they leave. This guide sets out a practical way to share IT safely in a multidisciplinary or room-rental clinic in BC. It is general information, not legal advice. Who is responsible for which records depends on your agreements and on your regulatory colleges, and you should confirm those points with your lawyer, your colleges and the BC Office of the Information and Privacy Commissioner.

Start with the question IT can't answer: whose records are they?

Before changing a single setting, the clinic owner and each practitioner should agree in writing on a few points:

  • Who is responsible for each practitioner's patient records: the clinic, the practitioner, or both in different ways. This shapes everything else.
  • Which system holds those records: the clinic's shared booking and charting account, or the practitioner's own.
  • What happens when a practitioner leaves: whether they take their patients' records with them, keep access for a period, or hand them over to the clinic.
  • Who pays for what: shared software seats, Microsoft 365 licences, devices and IT support.

These are business and professional questions, not technical ones. Your IT provider should build the set-up around whatever you decide rather than deciding for you. A common mistake is the reverse: the technology was set up first, usually as one big shared account, and the agreements were written later to fit it.

Two common set-ups, and what each means for IT

One shared clinic account

The clinic holds one booking and charting account, and every practitioner has a login inside it. Patients see one clinic brand, one online booking page and one front desk. It's simpler to run, and most cloud platforms used by BC clinics support this well.

The IT work is about permissions. Each practitioner should normally see their own schedule and the charts of the patients they treat, not the whole clinic's notes. Front desk staff need scheduling, contact details and payments, but usually not clinical notes. Only the owner or clinic manager should be able to create accounts, change permissions or export data. Before you rely on this model, check that your platform can actually restrict chart access by practitioner; not every product or plan does it the same way.

Separate accounts for each practitioner

Each practitioner runs their own booking and charting account and the clinic provides space, the front desk and the internet connection. Records are cleanly separated, and a departing practitioner simply takes their account with them.

The IT work here is about the shared environment. Front desk staff may need logins to several systems, which creates a temptation to keep a list of passwords on a sticky note. Each practitioner's own laptop or tablet now sits on the clinic network. And the clinic may have less visibility into how each practitioner protects their account. The clinic can't manage another business's software account, but it can set minimum standards for anything that connects to its network or front desk.

Many clinics end up with a mix: most practitioners in the shared account, with one or two (often a counsellor or a practitioner who also works elsewhere) on their own system. That's fine, as long as everyone knows which records live where.

The network: separate the clinic, the practitioners and the public

The biggest technical risk in a shared clinic is a single flat network where the front desk computer, the card terminal, an X-ray workstation, a renter's personal laptop and every patient's phone can all reach each other. If one device on that network is infected, everything on it is exposed.

A sensible layout for most multidisciplinary clinics has at least three separate networks:

  • Clinic network: clinic-owned computers, the front desk, printers and any imaging equipment. Only managed devices connect here.
  • Practitioner network: personal laptops, tablets and phones belonging to room renters and contractors. It reaches the internet and, if needed, the printer, but not clinic computers or each other.
  • Guest network: patients and visitors. Internet only, with nothing else reachable.

Card terminals ideally sit on their own segment as well. This is usually a configuration change on business-grade networking equipment rather than a large purchase, but it needs to be done carefully and documented. Change the Wi-Fi passwords when a practitioner leaves, and don't give the clinic network password to anyone who doesn't need it.

Shared computers, printers and the front desk

Shared equipment is where privacy slips usually happen in a busy clinic. A few habits make a big difference:

  • Individual sign-ins on shared computers. If a treatment room computer is used by different practitioners on different days, each should have their own Windows or Mac login, and the computer should lock after a short idle time. Never leave one practitioner's booking account open for the next person.
  • Nothing saved to the desktop. Intake forms, referral letters and reports should go into the right practitioner's system, not into a shared Downloads folder that the next user can browse.
  • Printer and scanner settings. A shared multifunction printer often stores scans, keeps an address book of email destinations and sometimes holds a copy of recent jobs. Turn on secure (PIN) release for confidential printing if your printer supports it, check where scan-to-email and scan-to-folder go, and clear the device's storage before it's returned at the end of a lease.
  • The front desk password problem. If reception staff need access to several practitioners' systems, give them their own named accounts in each one, and use a business password manager with shared vaults rather than a written list. When a staff member leaves, you then know exactly which accounts to change.
  • Fax and e-fax. If the clinic has one fax number, decide who sorts incoming faxes and how they reach the right practitioner without being left on a shared tray or in a shared inbox. Our secure e-fax guide for BC clinics covers the options.

Email: no shared practitioner inboxes

A single shared address such as info@ is useful for general enquiries, but clinical communication should not run through it. Each practitioner should have their own mailbox, either in the clinic's Microsoft 365 tenant or in their own business account, with multi-factor authentication (MFA) turned on. If clinic-domain mailboxes are provided to renters, agree who controls the mailbox and its contents when they leave, and write that into the agreement.

Shared clinics are also a target for invoice and payment fraud, because money moves between the clinic and each practitioner every month. Confirm any change to bank details by phone using a number you already have, never by replying to the email that asked for the change. Our clinic email security guide goes into more detail.

Personal devices: a short written standard

Room renters and contractors almost always use their own phones and laptops. You can't manage those devices the way you manage clinic computers, but you can require a minimum before they connect to the clinic's network or systems:

  • A supported operating system with updates turned on.
  • A screen lock, device encryption and, for phones, the ability to locate and wipe the device remotely.
  • Patient information kept inside the booking or charting app, not in photos, notes apps, personal email or personal cloud storage.
  • No shared family accounts on a device used for charting.
  • Prompt notice to the clinic if the device is lost or stolen.

Keep it to one page and have each practitioner sign it with their rental or contractor agreement. A short standard that people actually follow is worth more than a long policy nobody reads.

Arrivals and departures

Turnover is normal in shared clinics, and the most common security gap is the account that was never switched off. Use a written checklist for both ends.

When a practitioner starts: create their booking or charting account with the right role (not a copy of the owner's account), set up their mailbox with MFA, give them the practitioner Wi-Fi rather than the clinic network, add their name to the access list, and have them sign the device standard.

When a practitioner leaves: disable or remove their accounts on the agreed date, carry out the agreed records hand-over or transfer through a documented process rather than an informal export, remove calendar syncs and forwarding rules, change shared Wi-Fi and door codes, and update the access list. If the practitioner is taking records with them, make sure the transfer is complete before their access ends, and keep a note of what was transferred and when.

Once a quarter, compare the list of active accounts in every system against the people actually working in the clinic. It takes half an hour and catches most problems.

Backups when records live in several places

In a clinic with mixed set-ups, nobody may have a full picture of where patient information is stored. Make a simple list: the shared booking and charting platform, each practitioner's separate system, any X-ray or imaging computer, scanned documents, the fax inbox and any files on shared computers. For each one, write down who is responsible for backing it up and how a restore would be tested.

Cloud platforms keep their own backups, but those protect the vendor rather than the clinic. Find out how to get a full export of your data and how deleted records are recovered. For anything stored in the clinic itself, such as X-rays or scanned files, use the approach in our EMR backup best practices. If a chiropractor in the clinic has an X-ray system, it deserves particular care, as covered on our chiropractic IT page.

When something goes wrong

A shared clinic needs to know in advance who makes decisions during an incident. If a front desk computer is infected or a practitioner's account is taken over, the clinic owner and the affected practitioners may each have their own obligations to assess the breach and, where required, notify patients or regulators. Agree now who calls the IT provider, who contacts the software vendor, and how each practitioner will be told. Our clinic cyber incident response guide sets out the first 24 hours.

A quick checklist for shared clinics

  • Written agreement on who is responsible for each practitioner's records and what happens when they leave.
  • Booking and charting permissions set so practitioners see their own patients, and front desk sees what it needs.
  • Separate clinic, practitioner and guest networks, with card terminals kept apart.
  • Individual logins and auto-lock on every shared computer; nothing saved to shared desktops.
  • Printer scan destinations checked and secure release on for confidential printing.
  • Named accounts and a password manager for the front desk.
  • Individual mailboxes with MFA, and phone confirmation of any bank-detail change.
  • A signed one-page personal device standard.
  • Start and finish checklists, plus a quarterly account review.
  • A list of every place patient information is stored, and who backs up each one.

Frequently asked questions

Can room-renting practitioners use the clinic's Wi-Fi?

Yes, but give them a separate practitioner network that reaches the internet (and the printer, if needed) without being able to reach clinic computers, card terminals or each other's devices. Patients should be on their own guest network.

Should every practitioner be in one shared booking account?

It depends on your agreements. A shared account is simpler for patients and the front desk, but only works well if the platform can restrict chart access by practitioner and you've agreed what happens to records when someone leaves. Separate accounts keep records apart but make the front desk's job harder.

Who should pay for IT in a multidisciplinary clinic?

That's a business decision. Many clinics include basic IT (internet, Wi-Fi, front desk equipment) in the room rent and have practitioners pay for their own software seats and devices. Whatever you choose, write it into the rental or contractor agreement so it's clear when something breaks.
