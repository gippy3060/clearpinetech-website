Secure e-fax for BC clinics: what to check before you switch

Medical clinics · 3 November 2026

Fax is still how many BC clinics receive referrals, lab requests, specialist reports and pharmacy communication. Moving from a paper fax machine to e-fax can reduce lost pages and speed up filing, but only if the documents end up somewhere safe. A fax inbox that anyone can open, or a PDF forwarded to a personal email address, is a privacy problem you did not have before. This guide covers what to check before you switch, and what to do with the faxes once they arrive.

We are an IT provider, not your regulator or lawyer. Your college, your professional association and legal counsel can tell you what applies to your practice; this is the technical side.

Why paper fax is a quiet privacy risk

  • Pages sit in the output tray. Anyone walking past reception can read a referral or lab result.
  • Misdialled numbers. A wrong digit sends patient information to a stranger, and you may never know.
  • No record. A paper fax has no access log, no search and no backup.
  • Manual filing. Scanning paper into the chart takes staff time, and pages go missing between the tray and the record.

What e-fax changes, and what it does not

E-fax services send and receive faxes as digital files, typically delivered to a web portal, an email inbox or a shared folder. That removes the tray and adds logging, but it creates new questions. The fax is now a file that can be forwarded, downloaded, synced to a phone or stored in the wrong place. Most of the risk moves from the machine to the account and the storage.

Seven things to check before you switch

  1. Encryption. Ask whether faxes are encrypted in transit between your staff and the service, and encrypted when stored. Ask the vendor to say so in writing rather than relying on a marketing page. Note that traditional fax over a phone line between the sender and your service is outside your control; the protection applies to the part you can configure.
  2. Where documents are stored. Ask which country and which data centres hold your faxes, and whether that can be stated in your agreement. Clinics often need to be able to say where patient information is kept.
  3. Shared inbox, individual logins. Receiving faxes in one shared mailbox is convenient, but every person who opens it should sign in with their own account, with multi-factor authentication, so access can be traced and removed when someone leaves.
  4. Access by role. The front desk may need to route referrals but not read every lab report. Check whether the service can limit who sees which fax line or folder.
  5. Audit log. You should be able to see who sent, opened, downloaded or deleted a fax. If the service cannot tell you, it will be hard to investigate a complaint.
  6. Retention and deletion. Decide how long faxes stay in the service after they are filed into the EMR, and who deletes them. A fax inbox that keeps everything forever becomes a second, unmanaged patient record. Your regulator and your EMR vendor can tell you what record-keeping rules apply to the filed copy.
  7. Can you leave? Make sure you can export your faxes and fax numbers if you change service. Keeping your existing fax number (porting) is worth asking about before you sign.

Where the faxes should end up

An e-fax service is a delivery route, not a records system. The usual goal is that each document is filed to the right patient chart in your EMR and the copy in the e-fax service is then removed or archived under a stated policy. That makes the EMR the record you back up and protect. See our guide to EMR backup best practices for BC clinics, because anything filed into the chart is only as safe as that backup.

Many EMRs, including OSCAR Pro, Accuro and TELUS PS Suite, have their own document intake or integrate with fax services in different ways. Check with your EMR vendor which approach they support before choosing an e-fax product, and confirm that you will not need to re-key documents by hand.

Mistakes we would watch for

  • Forwarding faxes to a personal Gmail or phone. It is easy to set up, and it moves patient information outside your control. Block auto-forwarding to outside addresses.
  • One password for the whole office. When a staff member leaves, nobody changes it. Use individual accounts.
  • No cover-sheet or number check. Misdirected outgoing faxes remain possible with e-fax. Saved, verified contacts for the pharmacies and clinics you fax regularly cut that risk.
  • Phishing that imitates a fax notification. “You have a new fax” emails are a common lure. Train staff to open faxes from the portal or the filed chart, not from unexpected email links.
  • Ignoring the old machine. Wipe or destroy the storage in the old fax machine or multifunction printer before disposal; many keep copies of recent pages.

A simple switchover plan

  1. List every fax line and who uses it: reception, a physician, the pharmacy line, a billing line.
  2. Choose a service that meets the checks above and confirm in writing how it works with your EMR.
  3. Set up individual accounts with multi-factor authentication and role-based access.
  4. Port the existing numbers if possible, and run the old machine and the new service in parallel for a short time.
  5. Write a one-page procedure: who checks the inbox, how faxes are filed, how long copies are kept, what to do about a misdirected fax.
  6. Train staff, including how to spot a fake fax notification.
  7. Retire the old machine securely.

If a fax goes to the wrong place

Have a short written procedure before it happens: who is told, how the recipient is asked to delete or return the document, and who decides whether the incident needs to be reported. Your privacy officer, regulator or lawyer should set the reporting steps; the IT role is to pull the logs that show what was sent and to whom.
